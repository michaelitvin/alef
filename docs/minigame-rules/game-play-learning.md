# Game play and learning: difficulty, mistakes, hints and pace

## Difficulty

- Select questions to get a predicted mistake rate of 1 in 5.
  If no question reaches this rate, use the most difficult question available.
- Give recent answers more weight than old answers. Keep the history.
- Adapt the speed, but do not let the child see the change.
- Do not ask for the same thing twice in a row, and not twice in one set that the game plans ahead (a ladder). Choose the
  next question after his last answer, or remember what the plan already asked: the adaptive choice picks the same weak
  item again and again otherwise.
  Example (Hebrew Spell Quest): a ladder planned all its rungs at the start and asked "דָ" three times in a row.
- Make wrong answers only from content that the child learned. A trick must test the current skill, not show an unknown word.
  Example (clock): on level 1 (o'clock only), the answers included "ten to eleven".
- Make wrong answers clearly different from the right one. Do not offer two answers that differ only in a small detail.
  Example (clock): two 9:30 clocks, one with the short hand on the 9 (an impossible clock) and one half way to the 10.
  The parent: "too confusing, don't do this".
  Exception (English course): word choices may differ in one letter. Reason: reading `cat` and `cot` apart is the skill,
  and a choice that differs in the vowel only is the right test of it. Example: the ladder's rungs change one letter at
  a time (`lip`, `slip`), and the cauldron makes `cap` into `cape`. The rule stays for the Hebrew path and the clock.
- Record confusions by direction (the item asked, the item tapped). Put the confused item on the board when its pair is asked.
- Place a skilled child fast. Start with a placement check: ask a few items across the range early (each item once, or a
  search that jumps up after a fast right answer and down after a mistake), and let a clean result move him on at once. Keep the slow path for the other children. Check after each short part (a wave), not only at the end of a
  visit, and do not make the visits shorter while the check can still pass.
  Reason: a child who already knows the content gets bored in a long grind, and the next things to learn come late.
  Example (Hebrew Spell Quest): a child who knew all the nikkud needed about 40 tower answers before the minigames opened. Now the
  first 10 answers ask each mark once, and 10 right answers in a row (each mark's latest answer right) open the
  minigames. The parent wants a placement check for other things as well.
- Make the placement check look like normal play: the same rounds, hearts and points. Do not show or say that it is a
  check. Check the level again from time to time with a short probe upward, and stop the probe at the first mistake.
  Reason: the child must not feel tested, and his level changes between visits. A one-time check gets old.
  Example (Space Cat, the parent, 2026-11-20): "placement should be updated once in a while". A probe costs a heart like
  any mistake; every 5 play days, or after a visit in which he moved up 2 rungs fast, up to 3 probes test the rungs above
  his level.
- Turn all content on by default, and let the adaptive rules decide what the child sees. When he clearly does not know a
  part (he fails it again after an easier pause), hold it back for a while, then offer it again. Use the parent's
  switches to turn content off, not to turn it on.
  Reason: content behind a switch that is off never reaches the child, and the parent must not guess what he is ready
  for. Example (Space Cat, the parent, 2026-11-20): "The game should adapt. Hold it back for a while if the kid clearly
  doesn't know the item yet." Times and divide were off until the parent turned them on; now they are on, and a rung
  with 2 strikes is held for 2 play days (then 4, then 7).
- Do not let the child make a state that cannot exist. Make the controls move only between valid states. Do not show
  one either, also in decorations, demos and bonus games.
  Example (clock): the short hand moved freely while the long hand stayed, which made impossible times. Now the short hand
  steps an hour at a time, to the place that belongs with the long hand. In the bonus, the long and short hands stood
  still while the seconds hand swept round; now they turn with it.

## Explanations and mistakes

- Explain each new element when it first appears: say what it is and show it with an animation.
  Explain again after the child makes the same mistake several times. Then go back to short feedback.
  Reason: feedback alone does not teach a new idea, and the same mistake again and again shows that he does not understand it.
  Example (clock): at the first quarter past, the minute hand moves a quarter of the circle and the slice fills.
- Forgive a near miss one time.
- After a mistake or an explanation, do not go on by yourself. The child needs as much time as he wants to think.
  After a mistake, mark the right answer (and the wrong one) and let the child do the right move himself: drag the marked
  choice to its place or tap it. Then do what a right answer does on that screen (no point: it was a mistake). Do not show
  a next button for a mistake. If he waits, play the sound again. Keep an explanation on the screen when it ends, with a
  speaker that plays it again, until he taps next. Give the explanation of a mistake from time to time (not every time),
  after he has done the right move.
  Example (clock): after a mistake the next customer came at once, and an explanation went on to the question at once.
- Exception: in an action game (Hebrew Spell Quest: the broom flight), do not stop at a mistake. Flash, take a heart, mark the right
  answer for a moment, play its sound again, and go on. One wave of choices costs at most one heart: after its target is
  caught or a mistake happens, its other orbs fly past harmlessly. Start a new target only when the screen is empty, and
  announce it while the game goes on. Example (Hebrew Spell Quest): the parent: "make it more fluent, like an arcade game"; the old
  flight stopped at each mistake and new target.
- Let a moving answer reach the child before you judge it. Count a miss only when the answer has passed him, and end a
  round only when all the answers that are on the screen are done.
  Example (Hebrew Spell Quest): the broom said "missed" while the right letter still overlapped the wizard, and on the last round the
  letters still flying vanished.
- Do not repeat the instruction in each round. The demo teaches a screen; after that, a round plays only the sound to find.
  Play the instruction (and the sound again) only when the child has not answered for a while (Hebrew Spell Quest: 8 s).

## Hints

- Give hints that fit the question. Keep them behind a hint button. Show a hint by itself only when the child has not
  answered for a long time (Hebrew Spell Quest: 16 s). Do not show it at the start of a round or after a mistake: after a mistake the
  right answer glows and its sound plays.
  Example (clock): the quarters (the face cut in four, 00/15/30/45) for :15/:30/:45, and the minutes at each number for
  the other five-minute times; none on the hour.
  Example (tower): a tap on the twin mark (kamatz for patach) costs no heart. The wizard says "כִּמְעַט" and the twin's name.
- Let the child skip a hint or an explanation when he shows that he knows: he tries to pick. Keep the answers open
  while a hint plays. When he touches an answer (finger-down, before a drag or a tap ends) or puts the pen down to draw,
  remove the hint at once (its card, its glow, its pointing hand and its voice), and let the pick count. Keep the hint
  counted. Let a tap anywhere on an explanation that covers the screen end it.
  Reason: the hint covers his own try or takes his attention from it, and an explanation gets him stuck when he
  already knows the answer.

## Pace and repetition

- Keep the pace brisk. Between the answer and the next question, do not add fixed pauses longer than about 350 ms.
  Play sounds that belong together at the same time, and let a door bell ring while the round continues.
  Example (clock): a round had about 5 s of waiting after the answer, and the game felt slow.
- Keep the flow: do not repeat the same feedback many times in one encounter. Speak a praise line (a streak, the top of
  the ladder, a crystal) only the first two times of each kind; after that, play only its sound.
  Example (tower): boss hits before the last one get only a sound.
- Use a timer only where speed is the game. Do not use a clock for slow tasks, such as drawing.
  Example (Hebrew Spell Quest): only the broom flight has a time limit.

## Runs and sessions

- Calculate how many answers a usual run has before the hearts run out (at 1 mistake in 5). Make sure that every screen
  comes up in a usual run.
  Example (Hebrew Spell Quest): 3 hearts gave approximately 15 answers, and one cycle of the screens had approximately 25. Now the
  hearts fill again on each new screen, and the next run starts at the next screen.
- Make a new minigame available from the start. Do not lock it behind levels.
- Do not let progress go backwards. Keep the progress that the child can see.
- Pause the game when the tablet locks or the child switches to another app. Stop all sound, music too, and cover the
  game while it is paused. After the pause, continue with the same question.
- Start with a simple version. Add a mechanic only when the data shows a need.
  Example (tower): a simple random repeat of mistakes was sufficient for v1.
- A simple, repetitive drill is acceptable if it teaches the target skill and the child likes it.
  Example (tower): the child loved the tower. Monotony is the main open problem.
